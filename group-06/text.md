---
title: From the Machine to the Word
authors: Noyan and Alireza
---

*What does an AI data centre sound like, and who gets to describe it?* The noise is not abstract. It comes from **cooling systems**, and secondarily from the turbines and generators that power the site. We collected [107 published sources](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1z5Xn5UeWda6527Jl30bCQTBp5d59W9P35E3xEjl-Weo/edit?gid=516013045#gid=516013045) from 2018 to 2026 (news stories, reports, reviews and lawsuits) and coded each one by the machine it names, the pitch it gives the sound, the words it uses and who is speaking.

Description mostly fails. 59 sources never name a machine, 72 never say whether the sound is high or low, and 51 call it only {#d9480f|noise}. When a source does find a word, it is most often **hum** (35 sources), then tone (15) and whine (10). Naming the machine helps: 73% of sources that name it also find a sound word, against 36% of those that do not.

The same sound gets different words depending on the speaker. Residents compare: 23% of them say it sounds *like an aircraft*, and others reach for a vacuum cleaner. Institutions classify: 47% of them say {#1c5d99|tone}, and only they use terms such as infrasound. Everyone says hum.

The pitch moves over time. Early sources, many about one site in Chandler, Arizona, call the sound a high whine; later ones about large AI campuses describe a low hum, a rumble and finally infrasound. [[a measuring gap|Noise rules measure loudness in dB(A), a weighting that discounts low frequencies, the range people complain about most.|type=note;color=green]]

Our dataset has limits. 71 of the 107 entries are not yet checked against the original page, and among the 36 checked sources only 7 use no sound word, so the share of silent sources is likely lower than it looks. Several sources repeat the same story, and 77 of 107 are American.
